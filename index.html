The problem

Fifty states. Fifty different programs.

Through the big beautiful bill signed in 2025, CMS, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, is putting a total of $50 billion into rural health over five years, spread as $10 billion a year. The money flows through the states. Every state runs its own program, publishes its own documents, and announces its own winners on its own schedule.

The information exists, but it is scattered across plans, PDFs, spreadsheets and procurement pages. The Atlas brings it into one national view, so the search begins with organizations already funded for the problem your company solves.

Rural Health Atlas

The intelligence behind the work.

The Atlas turns public state documents into a connected view of awards, recipients and vendor fit. It shows where a company belongs without publishing the proprietary registry or its rankings.

01 / Awards

Read from the source.

I read the published funded awards and keep the source document behind each one. Every funded award in the Atlas names its recipient and cites the document it was read from, with the amount when the state discloses one.

02 / Organizations

Connected to the recipient.

The Atlas connects published recipients to a national database of more than 31,000 healthcare organizations and sites, from critical access hospitals and rural health clinics to EMS agencies, public health departments and tribal health entities.

03 / Vendor fit

Ranked against what you sell.

I use the scope of the funded awards to rank the fit between what you sell and the organizations already funded for related work, with the published award behind every match. The Atlas tracks close to 1,000 companies selling into rural health, in areas including workforce, behavioral health, EHR and health IT, remote patient monitoring, telehealth, care coordination, analytics, imaging and diagnostics, and EMS.

Why I built it

I’m Justin Brilliant.

I got into this while helping a dermatology health-tech company work out which rural organizations had funding that could pay for its technology. Nobody could tell me where the money had gone. A press release rounds the number, an aggregator runs weeks behind, and the awards themselves were sitting in state documents nobody was reading. It was confusing.

So I read them, and I compiled everything I found into one database. That database became Rural Health Atlas, and the whole thing has turned into my full-time job.

If you ever want to know where a number came from, ask and I’ll send you the document. And I’ll tell you when something isn’t a fit, rather than hand you a list that doesn’t hold up.
